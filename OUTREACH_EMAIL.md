I am following up with a more explicit formulation of why I am contacting you.

The convergence between the Science Silk Road project and my work on **Koali** is not accidental. We are responding, from different directions, to the same structural transformation.

As knowledge production becomes increasingly distributed across institutions, infrastructures, jurisdictions, technologies, and communities, a predictable set of problems follows: knowledge must cross organizational boundaries; expertise must be distinguished from authority; evidence must remain traceable; decisions must become executable; institutional learning must survive personnel turnover; and capabilities created through cooperation must be maintainable, transmissible, and reconfigurable rather than remaining permanently dependent on their original source.

I identified this problem two years ago and built Koali around it.

Koali is not a speculative framework. It is a sociotechnical architecture that is substantially designed and implemented, and is approaching functional deployment. Its purpose is broader than scientific cooperation: it addresses the general problem of transforming distributed knowledge and competence into durable collective agency and cumulative future capability.

The architecture follows the full chain:

**source → knowledge → interpretation → learning → competence → contribution → deliberation → judgment → decision → mandate → execution → result → evidence → memory → new capability**

Making that chain operational required explicit separation of concepts that are often compressed together:

**Evidence ≠ Advice**  
**Advice ≠ Authority**  
**Expertise ≠ Decision Authority**  
**Decision ≠ Execution**  
**Output ≠ Outcome ≠ Learning ≠ Capability Gain**  
**Access ≠ Mastery**  
**Training ≠ Capability Reproduction**  
**Repository Clone ≠ Functional Reproduction**

This is why I consider Koali relevant to the Science Silk Road at a deeper level than thematic overlap.

Your project examines how China’s expanding scientific and technological cooperation is reorganizing knowledge production, infrastructure, institutions, and international scientific relations.

Koali addresses the underlying systems problem:

> **How does knowledge, infrastructure, or expertise actually become a durable capability in another institutional context—and under what conditions can that capability later be maintained, transmitted, modified, and reproduced?**

For scientific cooperation, the relevant trajectory is not simply:

**technology transfer → capacity building**

but:

**infrastructure → access → practice → competence → institutionalization → transmission → reproduction → reconfiguration**

At every transition, authority and dependency can change.

An institution may operate an instrument without being able to repair it.  
It may analyze data without controlling access to them.  
It may possess expertise without setting the research agenda.  
It may receive extensive training without being able to train the next generation.  
It may reproduce software without being able to reproduce the surrounding institutional capability.

This produces a more precise empirical problem:

> **Which scientific capabilities are actually being formed, where do they reside, which forms of authority accompany them, which dependencies are produced simultaneously, and can the resulting configuration survive and reconfigure itself when people, technologies, funding, or partners change?**

Koali already treats these questions architecturally through provenance, differentiated authority, competence evidence, institutional memory, lifecycle continuity, modular composition, portability, and independent capability reproduction.

The Science Silk Road is therefore not simply an application domain that happens to resemble Koali. It is one major empirical manifestation of a broader transformation that Koali was designed to address.

This is not a mysterious convergence of unrelated ideas. The same historical pressures are making these questions unavoidable. Your work is theorizing and investigating that transformation in the global system of science. My work has approached it by asking what architecture is actually required for such systems to function without losing provenance, agency, institutional memory, contestability, or the capacity to become independently reproducible.

Koali also extends beyond the immediate scope of scientific cooperation. The same architecture addresses capability formation and reproduction across education, institutional governance, software, technical production, collective decision-making, and the preservation of organizational knowledge.
