## 2. Why ethnography matters for capability

Capability is often invisible in official documents. A project may report:

- people trained;
- equipment delivered;
- data produced;
- memoranda signed;
- laboratories opened.

But whether capability is durable may depend on practices that appear only through close observation:

- who is called when equipment fails;
- who knows undocumented procedures;
- who has passwords or vendor contacts;
- who can interpret anomalies;
- who can authorize changes;
- what happens when a trained staff member leaves;
- which workarounds emerge locally;
- which forms of knowledge never enter formal reports.

These are ethnographic questions.

## 3. Local agency and negotiated infrastructure

Zhou's work on African infrastructure and China–Africa relations is especially useful because it treats host actors as active participants rather than passive recipients.

Relevant work:

- *Western and Chinese Development Engagements in Uganda’s Roads Sector: An Implicit Division of Labour* — https://doi.org/10.1093/afraf/adac005
- *Unbinding Global China: Fieldnotes from a West African fishing port* — https://doi.org/10.1177/20438206251388520
- *Provincial variations and entrepreneurialism in the development of China’s Distant Water Fisheries (2011–2020)* — https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marpol.2022.105344

The capability framework should therefore ask not only what an external actor transfers, but how local institutions **appropriate, redirect, combine, resist, bypass, or repurpose** the resources and constraints they receive.

## 4. Bureaucratic capability as part of scientific capability

Science infrastructures depend on more than scientists. Procurement, customs, visas, budgeting, security, maintenance contracts, data rules, staffing, and inter-ministerial coordination can determine whether scientific activity persists.

This expands the unit of analysis from laboratory skill to **institutional capability ecology**.

## 5. Potential contribution to Zhou's research program

> Capability trajectories provide a structured way to connect everyday bureaucratic and infrastructural practices to longer-term questions of scientific agency, dependence, and institutional reproduction—without erasing local negotiation or reducing outcomes to state-level strategy.
